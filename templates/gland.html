◆ Cheat sheet — everything worth knowing before you paddle out

Swell direction — the headline

  • Best: SW to SSW, 240°–200° across the reef as a whole. This is Surfline's call and it matches every other source.
  • Split by which section each angle feeds: 165–190° outer window · 190–210° Speedies · 210–250° Moneytrees · 250–285° outer window.
  • More westerly (240°–270°) arrives more directly — less turning, less energy spent — so the biggest surf comes on a west-angled long-period swell. But the bathymetry focuses it into Kongs, and everything inside drops off noticeably.
  • More southerly (200°–180°) wants long period. Long lines slow down and bend back into the point — the famous "growers" that get bigger down the line as energy refracts in from the bay. On a straight south swell the wrap is over 90° to reach the inside.
  • Short-period south sweeps straight past the top of the point — the drop-off south of the point is over 1500 m, so it feels like the waves break "out to sea."
  • Straight south (~180°) is the most extreme angle G-Land can receive at all.
  • Ignore anything from the E/SE. That is trade windsea. It is offshore here and never becomes surf.

Tide

  • Surfline rates the spot "Tide: All" — but that is the reef as a whole, not any one section.
  • Speedies needs high water, and on the biggest days it wants the highest water of the month — a spring tide around the new or full moon.
  • Moneytrees prefers a higher tide, though the modern consensus is it is more fun shallower than the old high-tide-only orthodoxy.
  • Kongs is surfable at low "up to the limits of your courage."
  • Low tide is where people get hurt. The reef in front of Moneytrees is fully exposed at low.
  • Indonesian tides are genuinely complex — multiple amphidromes, strong diurnal inequality. Range at G-Land runs roughly 1.5–2.5 m.

Where the swell is born

  • Most Indonesian swell forms between the Cape of Good Hope and Heard Island — the deep Southern Indian Ocean.
  • The Kerguelen Islands region is the classic genesis zone. A low coalescing west of Kerguelen is the tell.
  • Typical travel time: 5–6 days from storm to reef.
  • Following a great-circle path, Indo can receive swell from the central South Atlantic, 15,000 km away — about 9 days of travel. That is the outer limit, and it is why a 16-day forecast is not fantasy here the way it is on an east coast.
  • Aim matters more than strength. A very strong storm aimed south of Java delivers power and long lines but poor consistency — few waves per set on the front end.

Live data — what exists

  • Wave buoys near G-Land: none. Not one, anywhere in the swell window.
  • NDBC 53401 (the only Indonesian NDBC station) — DART tsunami buoy, disestablished 2008.
  • Nearest tide gauge: IOC station at Port of Grajagan, 20 km away — but its sea-level sensor is dead; it only transmits battery voltage. Verified live, July 2026.
  • Nearest working gauge on this coast: Prigi, 292 km WNW, reporting now. Too far to borrow given Indonesian tidal complexity.
  • Christmas Island (AU), 970 km at bearing 79° — live radar gauge, sits on the westerly-swell corridor. Water level only, no waves.
  • So: tide and waves here are modelled, not measured. GFS-Wave and ECMWF-WAM for swell, a global tide model for water level, and the WA sentinel line above for the only real in-situ check.
